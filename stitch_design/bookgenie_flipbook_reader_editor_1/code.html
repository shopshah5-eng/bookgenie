The Glass Pavilion

I t was precisely twenty minutes before the equinoctial bells that Lord Vane noticed the copper needle trembling upon the marble pedestal. The pavilion was cold, its faceted octagonal ceilings condensing the salty breath of the tidal river beneath the cliffs. Not a breath of wind disturbed the brass sconces, yet the astrolabe spun with an unnatural, measured deliberation.

For three centuries, the archive had remained sealed under the papal charter of 1682. There were no maps left of the sunken gallery, only the cryptic journal entries attributed to Brother Alistair, who had vanished while attempting to measure the celestial parallax of the lost star.

The shadows lengthened across the worn limestone flags like ink poured through chilled water. When he finally reached into the recess behind the bronze astrolabe, his fingers brushed against cold vellum bound in salt-bleached goatskin. The inscription read simply: ‘To those who calculate what should only be endured.’

A tremor shuddered through the glazed ceiling. In the dark water below, something vast and ancient had shifted against the foundation stones, displacing the century-old ballast of forgotten ships.
